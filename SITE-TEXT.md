## 1. Profile & CV page → "Teaching philosophy"

Stitched word for word from your CV and your website ("Teaching approach", "Why I teach", "Teaching in the age of AI").

Learners do their best thinking when they feel known and respected. I start from what they already know, then set a challenge they reason through together in discussion, and end by applying the new skill to their own lives, so they leave with ideas they can call their own.

I plan most lessons as a three-part arc. First, I activate what learners already know, often through a vivid scenario or a playful dilemma resonant with their own lives. Then we move to an application phase, a scaffolded exercise that requires them to try something new and potentially fail. Finally, the newly developed skill is actualised by connecting it to some concrete project or real issue in the world that they can apply it to.

Reading difficult texts closely, building arguments and writing clear, well-structured essays are foundational skills in philosophy, and they make up a large part of what English Home Language asks of learners in Grades 10 to 12. In the classroom I rely on continual informal assessment: quick questioning, short written tasks and quizzes, which lets me adjust a lesson while it is still happening.

Generative AI can now write the kind of essay most classrooms use to teach and to assess. In my classrooms, thinking happens out loud: discussion, Socratic questioning, arguments defended on their feet. Writing is taught as thinking, drafted, talked about and revised with feedback. AI is on the table, openly, so that learners use it thoughtfully, and assessment rewards thinking that happens in front of me, in class.

I teach because I have seen what happens when students realise they can think for themselves, and that transformation carries into the rest of their lives. I bring fifteen years of teaching experience to any school or department, along with a collaborative spirit, high professional standards and genuine care. Above all, I keep learning from the people I teach.

## 3. Lesson 1 page (The Poet) → "Differentiation"

I wrote this from the strategies ticked in your WIL plan and your lesson notes. Not your words.

The device hunt is scaffolded so that every learner can contribute: the clue key narrows each search to a few lines and names how many devices to find, the freebie gives weaker groups an immediate first answer, and mixed groups let stronger readers model the explanation of a device for the others. Answers are scored per correct device, so partial success counts. The thought experiments and the dilemma give learners who struggle with abstract terminology a concrete way in, while the open discussion lets stronger learners stretch into the philosophical layer of the poem. Technology is left out so that no learner is disadvantaged by access to a device.
