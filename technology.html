Technology integration and teaching in under-resourced schools

A digital toolkit of five tools, one technology-enhanced English activity with its resource, and the same activity adapted for a classroom with unreliable or no internet, few or no learner devices and limited printing.

Digital toolkit

Five tools I used in my 2026 English classrooms, with the teaching purpose of each and one relevant limitation.

ToolTeaching purposeA limitation
KahootLive quizzes for revision and informal assessment. Every learner answers every question, the leaderboard keeps energy high, and the results show me at once which text or skill needs re-teaching.Needs internet and a device per learner, and the time pressure rewards speed over careful reading, so I use it for revision, not for teaching new material.
PowerPoint in presenter modeTeacher-led visual lessons and in-class games. For the film-language pub quiz I read each scenario aloud from the presenter notes before the answer slide appeared, so the slides paced the lesson instead of replacing me.Needs a projector and a laptop, and slides tempt a teacher to lecture; without deliberate pauses for discussion the lesson becomes passive.
Subtitled video clips (VLC and a projector)Putting faces and voices to a Shakespeare text before and after reading it: a subtitled supercut of The Tempest let learners see professional actors decode the Early Modern English they had struggled with.Film clips are copyrighted, so they can be shown in class but not shared or published, and a clip only works if the class has already done the reading.
Google DocsWriting and sharing lesson notes, resources and class-built lists (for example the "levelling up your English" resource list the Grade 11s built together), with one link for the whole cohort.Learners need an account and a connection to edit, and shared documents need clear rules or a few learners do all the work.
Generative AI assistant (ChatGPT or Claude)A planning aid for the teacher: drafting multiple-choice distractors, checking a lesson sequence for gaps, and producing a first draft of a resource that I then edit and verify against the text.It invents plausible but wrong quotations, line references and facts, so nothing it produces goes to learners unchecked, and learner work or personal information is never pasted into it.

Technology-enhanced English activity: the Tempest Gauntlet

Grade and context
Grade 11 English Home Language · CAPS Literature: drama, The Tempest Acts 1 and 2 · revision before the formal assessment
Learning outcomes
Learners recall and explain key plot points and character motives from Acts 1 and 2 (Ariel and Sycorax, Antonio and Sebastian's plot, Trinculo and Caliban, Prospero's treatment of Ferdinand), and justify an interpretation from the text.
Technology used
Kahoot on a projector, with one phone or tablet per learner
Time
15 to 20 minutes

Instructions

  1. Before class: import the quiz into Kahoot using the import file below (Kahoot's "Import spreadsheet" option), or open the questions-and-answers PDF to read from. Check the projector and the school Wi-Fi.
  2. Each learner joins on their own device, with a nickname. Show the game PIN on the projector.
  3. Explain the rules: every Gauntlet question counts double, 30 seconds per question, discuss before you tap, no notes.
  4. Run the four Gauntlet questions. After each one, Kahoot shows the correct answer and the spread of answers. Stop on any question that a large part of the class got wrong and go back to the scene: ask a learner who got it right to explain why from the text.
  5. Use the final leaderboard to award the Sleep Token, then tell the class which scene you will revisit next lesson based on the results.

The resource

Kahoot import file (Excel) All questions with answers (PDF) Sleep Tokens to print (PDF)

The Gauntlet is Round 6 of the full six-round revision quiz, which is presented as my assessment task on the Assessment page. The import file contains all six rounds, so the quiz can be run in full or one round at a time.

The same activity without internet, devices or printing

Same questions, same outcomes, same pace: only the scoring moves from the screen to the board.

What you need
A board and chalk or a marker; the one printed copy of the questions-and-answers PDF (or the teacher's own handwritten copy); one scrap of paper per learner for answers. Nothing is printed for learners.
Time
15 to 20 minutes

Instructions

  1. Give each learner a number and write the numbers across the top of the board as a scoreboard. Write "A B C D" large in the middle.
  2. Read each question and its four options aloud, twice. Learners have 30 seconds (count down on the board or by hand) to write their letter on their scrap of paper.
  3. On "show", every learner holds up their letter at the same time, so nobody can copy a neighbour. Give the points on the board: two per correct Gauntlet answer.
  4. Ask one learner who got it right to explain the answer from the text, and correct any misunderstanding before the next question.
  5. Total the board, award the Sleep Token (a line on the board works as well as a printed token), and note the question most learners got wrong for the next lesson.

The show-of-letters rule gives the same immediate, whole-class feedback that Kahoot does, and the teacher sees the spread of answers at a glance. Two learners can also be given two of the four options to argue for and against, which turns the quiz into a short debate when time allows.

Responsible AI, privacy, verification and accessibility

Responsible use. Generative AI can now write the kind of essay most classrooms use to teach and to assess. My answer is not to ban it but to teach what no model can do for a learner: reading closely, arguing honestly and thinking in dialogue. In my classrooms AI is on the table, openly. We talk about what it is good and bad at, so that learners use it thoughtfully, and assessment rewards thinking that happens in front of me, in class. With the Grade 11s the closing debate was whether AI should be deleted forever, argued through four ethical frameworks.

Privacy. I use AI tools as a planning aid for myself, never as a place for learner data. No learner names, marks, work or personal information go into any AI tool, and learners are not asked to create accounts on AI services for class work.

Verification. Anything an AI tool helps to draft is checked against the prescribed text before it reaches learners: quotations and line references are confirmed in the book, facts are checked against the textbook or a reliable source, and distractors in multiple-choice questions are tested so that only one answer is defensible.

Accessibility. Every digital activity here has a version that needs no device, no connection and at most one printed page, so no learner is excluded by access. Video clips are shown with subtitles, slides use large text and high contrast, and quiz questions are read aloud as well as shown. Learners who need more time or a quieter setting can do the same quiz on paper at their own pace.
