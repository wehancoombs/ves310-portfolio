Responsible AI

Four rules I teach and plan by.

Use

AI is on the table, openly.

  • I teach what no model can do: close reading, honest argument, thinking in dialogue
  • Assessment rewards thinking done in class, in front of me
  • Grade 11 debate: should AI be deleted forever?

Privacy

A planning aid for me, never a place for learner data.

  • No learner names, marks or work in any AI tool
  • No AI accounts required of learners
  • Kahoot with team names, not real names

Verification

Checked against the book before learners see it.

  • Quotations and line references confirmed in the text
  • Facts checked against the textbook or a reliable source
  • Quiz distractors tested: only one defensible answer
  • AI use stated on the resource

Accessibility

Every activity has a no-device version.

  • No internet, no device, at most one printed page
  • Subtitled clips; large, high-contrast slides
  • Questions read aloud as well as shown
  • Paper version for learners who need more time

Tool 1 of 5

Kahoot

Teaching purpose

Live team quizzes for revision and informal assessment. Every learner answers every question, the leaderboard keeps energy high, and the results show me at once which text or skill needs re-teaching.

A limitation

It needs internet and a device per team, and the time pressure rewards speed over careful reading, so I use it for revision, not for teaching new material.

Tool 2 of 5

PowerPoint in presenter mode

Teaching purpose

Teacher-led visual lessons and in-class games. For the film-language pub quiz I read each scenario aloud from the presenter notes before the answer slide appeared, so the slides paced the lesson instead of replacing me.

A limitation

It needs a projector and a laptop, and slides tempt a teacher to lecture; without deliberate pauses for discussion the lesson becomes passive.

Tool 3 of 5

Subtitled video clips

Teaching purpose

Putting faces and voices to a Shakespeare text before and after reading it. A subtitled supercut of The Tempest, played through VLC on the projector, let learners see professional actors decode the Early Modern English they had struggled with.

A limitation

Film clips are copyrighted, so they can be shown in class but not shared or published, and a clip only works if the class has already done the reading.

Tool 4 of 5

Google Docs

Teaching purpose

Writing and sharing lesson notes, resources and class-built lists, for example the “levelling up your English” resource list the Grade 11s built together, with one link for the whole cohort.

A limitation

Learners need an account and a connection to edit, and shared documents need clear rules or a few learners do all the work.

Tool 5 of 5

Generative AI assistant

Teaching purpose

A planning aid for the teacher: drafting multiple-choice distractors, checking a lesson sequence for gaps, and producing a first draft of a resource that I then edit and verify against the text.

A limitation

It invents plausible but wrong quotations, line references and facts, so nothing it produces goes to learners unchecked, and learner work or personal information is never pasted into it.

Responsible AI · 1 of 4

Responsible use

Generative AI can now write the kind of essay most classrooms use to teach and to assess. My answer is not to ban it but to teach what no model can do for a learner: reading closely, arguing honestly and thinking in dialogue. In my classrooms AI is on the table, openly. We talk about what it is good and bad at, so that learners use it thoughtfully, and assessment rewards thinking that happens in front of me, in class. With the Grade 11s the closing debate was whether AI should be deleted forever, argued through four ethical frameworks.

Responsible AI · 2 of 4

Privacy

I use AI tools as a planning aid for myself, never as a place for learner data. No learner names, marks, work or personal information go into any AI tool, and learners are not asked to create accounts on AI services for class work. Quiz platforms such as Kahoot are used with team names, not real names.

Responsible AI · 3 of 4

Verification

Anything an AI tool helps to draft is checked against the prescribed text before it reaches learners: quotations and line references are confirmed in the book, facts are checked against the textbook or a reliable source, and distractors in multiple-choice questions are tested so that only one answer is defensible. Where AI was used in preparing a resource, the resource says so.

Responsible AI · 4 of 4

Accessibility

Every digital activity here has a version that needs no device, no connection and at most one printed page, so no learner is excluded by access. Video clips are shown with subtitles, slides use large text and high contrast, and quiz questions are read aloud as well as shown. Learners who need more time or a quieter setting can do the same quiz on paper at their own pace.

Learners do their best thinking when they feel known and respected.

1Known

Start from what they already know: a vivid scenario, a playful dilemma from their own lives.

2Challenge

A scaffolded exercise they reason through together, where trying something new and failing is allowed.

3Own

Apply it to a real issue in their world, so they leave with ideas they can call their own.

What English asks

Close reading, argument and clear, well-structured essays: the core of English Home Language in Grades 10 to 12. Quick questioning, short tasks and quizzes let me adjust a lesson while it is still happening.

In the age of AI

AI can write the standard essay. So thinking happens out loud, writing is taught as thinking, drafted and revised, and assessment rewards what happens in front of me, in class.

Grade 11English Home LanguageLiterature · poetry28 August 202645 minutes98/100 observed lesson

Would you give up your imagination to always know the truth?

Theseus’s speech on the lunatic, the lover and the poet, from A Midsummer Night’s Dream. “Poiesis” goes on the board before the learners walk in. A time-crunch device hunt in groups with a clue key on the board; then poiesis, a skateboarding giraffe, a square triangle, and the dilemma.

The lesson at a glance · tap a stage

5Hook10Device hunt15Poiesis15The dilemma
  1. Hook. A break from assessments: one learner reads, I read for rhythm, gut reactions, then the plot of the play in two minutes.
  2. Device hunt. Groups race the clock to find two devices every few lines, following the clue key. Enjambment is a freebie; scores are held back.
  3. Poiesis. Speech is everyday magic: a skateboarding giraffe appears in every head. Line by line, the groups supply the held-back answers.
  4. The dilemma. Xenia, residue, a square triangle, and a vote: truth or imagination? Cut right before the bell.

Grade 10English Home LanguageWriting · the literary essay5 August 202645 minutes

Whose fault is it?

The night after the murder: horses that broke their stalls and ate each other. From there to Jacobean witch panic and the divine right of kings, then the question every essay has to answer. The class divides the blame between the witches, Lady Macbeth and Macbeth himself and defends the split from the text, and we close with the PEEL method for an evidence-based paragraph.

The lesson at a glance · tap a stage

10Hook10Ambition20The blame debate5PEEL
  1. Hook. Act 2 Scene 4 read aloud and unpacked: darkness at noon, horses eating each other, witchcraft panic, nature in revolt against a king-killer.
  2. Ambition. What is your biggest ambition, and what would you do to get it? Would you betray a friend if you could get away with it, and a partner pushed you?
  3. The blame debate. Three places for the blame: the witches, Lady Macbeth, Macbeth. The class votes and argues from the text, then we turn to deception and guilt.
  4. PEEL. Point, Evidence, Explain, Link: the paragraph structure for the essay, with the school’s rules.

Feedback

The feedback is immediate and whole-class: after every question Kahoot shows the correct answer and how many teams chose each option, and I stop to explain any question that more than a third of the room got wrong, going back to the line of the poem or the scene of the play. The round scores tell me which text or skill needs re-teaching before the formal assessment, and I open the next lesson with that. Teams talk through every answer before they commit, so learners also give each other feedback as they go.

Differentiation

Teams are mixed, so that stronger readers explain their reasoning to weaker ones and every learner takes part without being exposed. Questions are graded within each round from recall (which play, which device) to interpretation (irony, structure), and the double-point bonus questions stretch the strongest learners without punishing the rest. Thirty seconds per question is enough for discussion but short enough to keep attention. The quiz needs no reading of long passages, which helps learners who read slowly, and the Sleep Token prize keeps the stakes playful. For a learner who cannot take part on a device, the same quiz runs on paper.

Grade 11 English Home Language · CAPS Literature: drama, The Tempest Acts 1 and 2 · 15 to 20 minutes

Outcomes. Learners recall and explain key plot points and character motives from Acts 1 and 2 (Ariel and Sycorax, Antonio and Sebastian’s plot, Trinculo and Caliban, Prospero’s treatment of Ferdinand), and justify an interpretation to their team from the text.

Instructions

  1. Before class. Import the quiz into Kahoot with the import file below (Kahoot’s “Import spreadsheet” option), or open the questions-and-answers PDF to read from. Check the projector and the school Wi-Fi.
  2. Teams of three or four, one device per team. Show the game PIN on the projector.
  3. Rules: every Gauntlet question counts double, 30 seconds per question, discuss before you tap, no notes.
  4. Run the four Gauntlet questions. After each one Kahoot shows the correct answer and the spread of team answers. Stop on any question a third of the room got wrong and go back to the scene: ask a team that got it right to explain why from the text.
  5. Use the final leaderboard to award the Sleep Token, then say which scene you will revisit next lesson based on the results.

Same questions, same outcomes, same team discussion: only the scoring moves from the screen to the board.

What you need

A board and chalk or a marker; one printed copy of the questions-and-answers PDF, or the teacher’s own handwritten copy; one scrap of paper per team. Nothing is printed for learners.

Same outcomes

Learners recall and explain the key plot points and motives of Acts 1 and 2, and justify an interpretation to their team from the text.

Instructions

  1. Write the team names across the top of the board as a scoreboard. Write “A B C D” large in the middle.
  2. Read each question and its four options aloud, twice. Teams have 30 seconds (count down on the board or by hand) to agree and write their letter on their scrap of paper.
  3. On “show”, every team holds up its letter at the same time, so no team can copy another. Give the points on the board: two per correct Gauntlet answer.
  4. Ask one team that got it right to explain the answer from the text, and correct any misunderstanding before the next question.
  5. Total the board, award the Sleep Token (a line on the board works as well as a printed token), and note the question most teams got wrong for the next lesson.

The show-of-letters rule gives the same immediate, whole-class feedback that Kahoot does, and the teacher sees the spread of answers at a glance. When time allows, teams can be given two of the four options to argue for and against, which turns the quiz into a short debate.
